Herbert Grubel and The Case for the Amero

The amero is twenty-six years old. In September 1999 — the same year the euro went virtual — Professor Herbert Grubel of Simon Fraser University published a Fraser Institute monograph that gave the idea its name and its first rigorous economic argument.

This page is the canonical reference for that paper. It records who Grubel was, what he actually argued, how the proposal travelled through Canadian and American policy circles, and why the 1999 case still anchors every serious discussion of North American monetary integration in 2025.

Who was Herbert Grubel?

Herbert Gunter Grubel was born on February 26, 1934 in Frankfurt, Germany. He took his B.A. at Rutgers University in 1958 and his Ph.D. at Yale, before migrating to Canada in 1972, where he eventually became Professor Emeritus of Economics at Simon Fraser University and a Senior Fellow at the Fraser Institute. He also served as a Canadian federal Member of Parliament for the Reform Party from 1993 to 1997.

Grubel is best known to economists not for the amero but for the Grubel–Lloyd Index, the standard measure of intra-industry trade he co-developed in the 1970s. The index is taught in undergraduate trade courses worldwide and remains in active use at the WTO and OECD. Across his career he produced 27 books and more than 130 peer-reviewed articles in international trade, finance, and immigration economics.

The amero proposal arrived late in his career — and it drew on every part of it. Grubel's familiarity with intra-industry trade gave him hard data on how integrated the three North American economies already were; his work on monetary policy gave him the theoretical tools; his role at the Fraser Institute gave him the platform.

The Argument, in Five Sentences

  1. The Canadian, US, and Mexican economies meet the textbook conditions of an Optimum Currency Area: synchronized business cycles, deep labor mobility (in two of the three pairs), and the fiscal capacity to converge.
  2. Cross-border currency conversion in North America imposed annual deadweight costs that Grubel estimated above US$20 billion — a tax on every transaction, paid to no one productively.
  3. Independent monetary policy was already a partial fiction: the Bank of Canada tracked the Federal Reserve closely, and the Mexican peso was repeatedly destabilized by USD shocks.
  4. A common currency — the amero — would eliminate the conversion tax, anchor expectations, and pull the three economies onto a shared price level, just as the euro had begun to do for the eleven Eurozone founding members.
  5. The political obstacle, not the economic one, was the binding constraint: Washington had no incentive to share monetary sovereignty, and Ottawa and Mexico City would have to accept that fact.
"The introduction of the amero would be in the best interests of all three countries." — Robert Pastor, Vice-Chair, Independent Task Force on North America, in Toward a North American Community (Institute for International Economics, 2001)

Reception

The paper landed with measurable impact for an academic monograph. Mexican President Vicente Fox publicly expressed support for a continental currency in the early 2000s. The Canadian Senate took it up in academic and parliamentary discussion. In 1999, Grubel participated in a "Round Table on a North American Currency" alongside Jack Carr, Thomas Courchene, John Crow, and Bernard Wolf, the proceedings of which were published in the Canadian Parliamentary Review (22:2, pp. 5–13).

US officials, however, were unmoved. The dominant Treasury position was that the United States already enjoyed every benefit a monetary union could deliver — and would surrender control to gain nothing. Grubel anticipated this in the paper itself; the political section is bracingly realistic about Washington's incentives.

Grubel returned to the proposal four years later in The Merit of a North American Monetary Union, a chapter in Salvatore, Dean and Willett's edited volume The Dollarization Debate (2003). The argument was substantively unchanged. The political environment had not improved.

Where the 1999 Case Fell Short

Two limitations of the original paper are now clear, and they are exactly the limitations the digital amero is designed to address.

First, the technology assumption. Grubel wrote in 1999. He could only imagine a paper-and-electronic-bank-balance currency on the euro template — issued by a new tri-national central bank, with all the political baggage that implies. Blockchain settlement, programmable stablecoins, and the modern XRP ledger did not exist as practical infrastructure. The 2025 Digital Amero proposal in Che-Sun Lee's book replaces a single new central bank with a distributed bridge architecture, preserving each nation's sovereign currency while sharing a settlement layer.

Second, the regulatory assumption. In 1999 there was no legal framework for issuing a digital, fiat-backed currency at scale. As of July 2025, the United States has the GENIUS Act — the first federal stablecoin statute — and the EU has MiCA. Grubel's amero needed a treaty; the digital amero needs a compliance regime, which already exists.
