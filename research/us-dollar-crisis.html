The US Dollar Crisis — Why North America Needs a Hedge

For eighty years, the United States dollar has been the world's reserve currency, the unit in which oil is priced, in which sovereign debt is held, and in which sanctions bite. None of that ends suddenly. All of it is eroding measurably — and the curve is steeper than most policy is built for.

The data, plainly

USD share of global FX reserves71.0% (2001) → 58.4% (2014) → 54.8% (2025)
USD share of SWIFT messaging~49.2% (2025), with CNY share rising rapidly off a small base
USD share of currency-backed stablecoins~99% — a digital-rails counter-trend that is itself a vulnerability
US federal debt held by the public$28T+ and rising on the current fiscal trajectory
BRICS+ share of global GDP (PPP)~36% — a coalition with both the size and the motive to build alternatives

None of these numbers in isolation forecasts a collapse. Together they describe a slow, durable rerating. The reserve share is now near where the British pound's was in the late 1940s — not yet displaced, but visibly past its peak.

What's driving the trend

  1. Sanctions architecture. The freezing of Russian central-bank reserves in 2022 was a watershed: holders of dollar reserves now price the political risk of holding them, and that price is no longer zero.
  2. BRICS infrastructure. mBridge (BIS-supported until 2024, since taken independent), CIPS, and BRICS Pay are operational rails. Their volume is small. Their existence is the point.
  3. The fiscal arithmetic. US debt service is now the third-largest line item in the federal budget. Foreign holders read the same charts US economists do.
  4. Energy decoupling. Oil-pricing in non-USD has moved from theoretical to incremental: Saudi-China yuan crude trades, ruble-payment energy contracts, multi-currency LNG settlements.
  5. The digital alternative. A reserve currency must be both a store of value and a payment medium. China's e-CNY and Europe's digital euro project are first credible competitors on the payments side in fifty years.

Why a North American hedge — not a replacement

The honest answer: the dollar is not going to be replaced this decade. The danger is not displacement; it is gradual relegation, which is a slower and stranger problem. A relegated reserve currency loses pricing power, exorbitant privilege, and fiscal slack — but keeps every domestic obligation. That is the worst of every world.

A continental hedge does three things. It diversifies the settlement base across the USMCA bloc. It captures supply-chain trade flows that would otherwise route through external alternatives. And it does so without asking the US to surrender the dollar's domestic role — because a digital amero, by design, sits beside the dollar rather than on top of it.

"The United States does not need to defend the dollar's reserve role at every corridor. It needs to control the corridors that matter most. North America is the corridor that matters most." — Lee, The Amero: Digital Bridge to the Fintech Future, ch. 14

What this implies for policy

  • Stablecoin policy is now reserve-currency policy. The GENIUS Act's requirement that issuers hold US Treasuries is, in effect, a private-sector reinforcement of dollar demand.
  • Settlement architecture matters as much as monetary architecture. The corridor that wins the next decade is the one with the lowest friction, not the highest reserves.
  • Time pressure is real. A ten-year ECB launch window for the digital euro is a luxury North America cannot match if the trend lines hold.
