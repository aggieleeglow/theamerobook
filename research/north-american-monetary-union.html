The North American Monetary Union

Five hundred million people, the world's most integrated supply chain, and three currencies that quietly tax every trade between them. The economic case for a continental currency was first written in 1999. The technology to do it differently arrived in 2025.

Why this question is back

The North American Monetary Union (NAMU) is no longer the academic curiosity it was when Herbert Grubel published his Fraser Institute paper in September 1999. Three forces have collapsed the distance between the proposal and its preconditions.

One: the US dollar's reserve share has fallen from 71% in 2001 to 54.8% in 2025, and BRICS has begun to build credible settlement alternatives. The cost of doing nothing is now visible on a chart.

Two: the GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, is the first comprehensive federal stablecoin framework in the United States. The legal scaffolding for a fiat-backed digital currency exists for the first time.

Three: blockchain settlement, and in particular the XRP ledger, makes it technically feasible to share liquidity across borders without surrendering monetary sovereignty. The euro template — one new central bank, one new currency, one shared fiscal fate — is no longer the only template.

Is North America an Optimum Currency Area?

Robert Mundell's 1961 OCA framework asks four questions of any candidate region: are business cycles synchronized; is there labor mobility; is there fiscal capacity for transfers; is intra-regional trade large enough to justify the loss of independent monetary policy?

Synchronized cyclesCanada–US correlation of GDP growth exceeds 0.85 since NAFTA. Mexico–US correlation strengthened sharply post-2008.
Labor mobilityAsymmetric and politically constrained — the binding weakness of the NAMU case, especially at the US–Mexico border.
Trade integrationOver 80% of Canadian exports go to the United States. USMCA-zone intra-industry trade is among the highest in the world.
Fiscal transfer capacityLimited at the federal level, but informal transfers via FDI, supply-chain investment, and remittances are deep and persistent.

The honest answer: stronger than the original 1999 Eurozone on trade and cycle synchronization, weaker on labor mobility. A continental currency designed for North America has to be designed around that reality — not pretend it does not exist.

What North America must learn from the Eurozone

The euro is the single most-studied monetary union in history, and most of what it teaches is about what to avoid.

  1. Don't separate monetary union from fiscal sovereignty without a backstop. The Greek crisis of 2010–2015 was the bill for that decoupling. A NAMU design must specify the lender-of-last-resort and the rules for asymmetric shocks before issuance, not after.
  2. Don't bind nations to a single price level if their productivity diverges. Spain and Germany did not converge; they diverged. A bridge-layer design — where each country keeps its currency and the amero is the settlement medium — preserves the adjustment valve.
  3. Don't underestimate political fragility. Brexit was, in part, a referendum on monetary integration the United Kingdom never even joined. Continental currencies survive only with continuous democratic consent.
  4. Build a regulatory framework first. The euro had a treaty before it had a currency. North America has, in the GENIUS Act and in MiCA, the regulatory architecture to issue a digital amero before any treaty is required.
  5. Don't ignore the digital reality. The euro was designed as a paper currency in 1999. The amero, in 2025, can be issued natively as programmable digital money — saving a generation of retrofitting.

The Digital Amero: how it differs from Grubel's 1999 design

Grubel's 1999 proposal envisioned a single tri-national central bank issuing a paper-and-balance amero on the euro template. The version proposed in The Amero: Digital Bridge to the Fintech Future (Lee, 2025) keeps the economic argument and rebuilds the architecture.

Issuance1999: single new central bank · 2025: regulated stablecoin issuers under GENIUS Act / OSFI / CNBV oversight
National currencies1999: abolished · 2025: retained — the digital amero is a bridge layer
Settlement1999: SWIFT + correspondent banking · 2025: XRP ledger + permissioned DLT
Programmability1999: none · 2025: native — smart contracts for trade finance, escrow, supply-chain payments
Political ask1999: Treaty + lost sovereignty · 2025: regulatory MOU + retained sovereignty
"A digital bridge currency is the smallest political ask that delivers the largest economic gain. It does not require Washington to share monetary sovereignty. It only requires Washington, Ottawa, and Mexico City to agree on a settlement standard." — Lee, The Amero: Digital Bridge to the Fintech Future, ch. 18

Frequently asked questions

Is the amero a real proposal or a conspiracy theory?

It is a real, twenty-six-year-old academic proposal. It also has a parallel conspiracy-theory life on the internet, often entangled with NESARA/GESARA. We cover the difference in NESARA & GESARA — Compared to the Amero.

Would the amero replace the US dollar?

In the 1999 design: yes. In the 2025 digital design: no. The dollar, the loonie, and the peso remain. The amero is the settlement layer between them.

Has any government formally endorsed it?

Mexican President Vicente Fox publicly supported a continental currency in the early 2000s. No US administration has. As of 2025, no formal NAMU treaty exists or is under negotiation; the live policy work is on stablecoin regulation and CBDC standards.
