Myth vs. Academic Record

NESARA, GESARA, and the Real Amero Proposal

Search for "amero" online and you will find two parallel universes. One is a quarter-century of peer-reviewed economics. The other is a long-running internet conspiracy known as NESARA / GESARA. This page is the honest map of the difference — because confusing the two damages policy debate, and damages people.

Short version. The amero is a serious academic proposal first published by Professor Herbert Grubel at the Fraser Institute in September 1999. NESARA / GESARA is a conspiracy theory with no legislative existence, no academic citations, and a persistent record of harm. They are not the same thing and the amero proposal does not depend on, endorse, or reference NESARA in any way.

What NESARA actually claims

NESARA — the National Economic Security and Reformation Act — is described by its proponents as a secret 2000 US law that would forgive all debts, abolish the IRS, return the dollar to a precious-metal standard, and trigger a global "currency reset." GESARA is its global extension. Proponents claim the law was signed but has been suppressed by hidden actors.

None of this is supported by the public record. There is no NESARA in the US Code. Congress.gov returns no matching legislation. The proposal first appeared in the late 1990s in connection with a private firm called the Omega Trust, which was the subject of a federal fraud prosecution that resulted in convictions. Subsequent academic and journalism reviews — by RAND, by the Anti-Defamation League, by mainstream financial press — have found NESARA to be a sustained financial-conspiracy narrative.

What the amero actually is

The amero is a tri-national currency proposal first formally argued by Professor Herbert Grubel in The Case for the Amero: The Economics and Politics of a North American Monetary Union, published by the Fraser Institute as a Critical Issues Bulletin in September 1999. The paper is catalogued in WorldCat (ID 46924208), discussed in the Canadian Parliamentary Review (1999, vol. 22, no. 2), endorsed in part by Robert Pastor in his 2001 book Toward a North American Community (Institute for International Economics), and updated by Grubel himself in 2003 in Salvatore, Dean and Willett's The Dollarization Debate.

Twenty-six years of academic literature, parliamentary discussion, and policy think-tank work sits behind the proposal. The 2025 Digital Amero book (Che-Sun Lee) extends that record into the era of blockchain settlement and the GENIUS Act.

Why this distinction matters

It matters for three reasons. First, citizens deserve accurate information. The amero is a real policy question with real trade-offs; conflating it with NESARA gives people a false reason to dismiss it, and a false reason to embrace it. Second, the conspiracy narrative has a documented history of attaching itself to investment scams. Third, serious monetary reform — of which the digital amero is one credible variant — needs a public conversation built on evidence, not on stories.

If you came here looking for NESARA

Read the actual academic record. The 1999 Grubel paper is the place to start. The full NAMU case is the place to go next. The amero is a more interesting idea than the conspiracy, and it does not require believing anything that is not in the public record.
