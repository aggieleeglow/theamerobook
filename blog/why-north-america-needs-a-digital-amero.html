Why North America Needs a Digital Amero — Now

By Che-Sun Lee · Foundations

A monetary proposal does not become urgent on its own. It becomes urgent because facts on the ground catch up to it. The amero, after twenty-six dormant years, has had five different categories of fact catch up to it in the last twenty-four months.

1. The fiscal arithmetic stopped being theoretical

For most of the 2000s and 2010s, US fiscal warnings were a genre rather than a forecast. That ended somewhere in 2024. Federal interest expense crossed defense spending. The reserve-currency tailwind, which has subsidized US borrowing for eighty years, is now visibly thinner: the dollar's share of global FX reserves has fallen from 71% in 2001 to 54.8% in 2025. A continental hedge is not a rejection of the dollar; it is a way of writing a put option on it.

2. The technology stopped being speculative

When Grubel wrote his 1999 paper, "digital currency" meant a balance in a bank database. The amero, on his template, had to look like the euro: a single new central bank, a single new physical currency, a single new fiscal compact. The political ask was enormous, which is why the proposal stalled.

In 2025, the equivalent of that infrastructure already runs in production. Public ledgers settle in seconds at fractional-cent fees. Stablecoins move $15 trillion a year. Tokenized deposits and central-bank wholesale-CBDC pilots have moved from white papers to Pontes/Appia-style live infrastructure at the ECB. The technical question is not whether a continental settlement layer can be built. It is which standards it should obey.

3. The geopolitics stopped being polite

The 2022 freezing of Russian central-bank reserves was the moment dollar-holders started pricing political risk into reserve composition. China's e-CNY went from Olympic novelty to corridor reality. BRICS settlement infrastructure (mBridge, CIPS, the proposed BRICS Pay) moved from press-release stage to operational. Saudi yuan-denominated crude trades exist. None of this displaces the dollar tomorrow. All of it pressures the dollar's pricing power, every day.

A continental hedge is the cheapest insurance North America can buy against the slow version of this scenario.

4. The regulatory ground finally settled

The single most important development in the amero story since 1999 is not technological. It is legislative. The GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, is the first comprehensive federal stablecoin framework in US history. Combined with the Trump administration's January 2025 executive order prohibiting a Federal Reserve retail CBDC, it draws the lane: private, GENIUS-chartered, fully reserved, audited stablecoins will be the legal vehicle for digital dollars. That is exactly the lane a digital amero proposes to occupy at the corridor level.

The 1999 proposal needed a treaty. The 2025 proposal needs three regulatory regimes that already largely exist: GENIUS in the US, OSFI/Bank of Canada in Canada, and CNBV in Mexico. Mutual recognition replaces constitutional change.

5. The historical lesson finally landed

The Eurozone has now run for a quarter-century. The Greek crisis is fifteen years behind us. We know which design choices were fragile (no shared fiscal capacity, no distinct adjustment channels, no exit mechanism) and which were durable (transparent monetary governance, open-market access, regulatory unity). The Eurozone is now a teacher rather than a hypothetical. The Digital Amero design uses that lesson to keep what works (regulatory and settlement integration) and discard what does not (mandatory currency abolition, fiscal lock-in).

"The 1999 amero asked North America to become Europe. The 2025 amero asks it to become better than Europe — by learning from twenty-five years Europe spent learning."

What "now" actually means

This is not a call for a treaty next quarter. It is a claim about timeline pressure. The realistic milestones over the next thirty-six months are:

  • GENIUS-charter rulemaking finalized; first wave of compliant US stablecoin issuers operational.
  • Mutual-recognition discussions between US, Canadian, and Mexican regulators on stablecoin reserve standards.
  • Wholesale-CBDC pilots at the Bank of Canada and Banco de México on the ECB Pontes/Appia template.
  • Corridor pilots: USMCA-zone trade transactions settled across a public bridge ledger using GENIUS-compliant stablecoins.
  • A formal continental policy position — agnostic on the political union question, decisive on the settlement-standard question.

None of this requires accepting the full 1999 amero. None of it forecloses it either. It is the path that delivers most of the economic gain for the smallest political ask — and it is finally available.
