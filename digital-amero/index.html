Digital Currency & Monetary Integration

Digital Amero: A Strategic Framework for the Future of Money

A serious policy thought experiment exploring whether North America needs coordinated digital financial infrastructure for the decades ahead.

The Concept

What Is the Digital Amero?

The Digital Amero is not a prediction. It is not a product, and it is not a political campaign. It is a strategic framework: a structured way of asking whether the three largest economies in North America—Canada, the United States, and Mexico—would benefit from coordinated digital financial infrastructure.

The concept builds on decades of academic discussion about monetary cooperation in North America. Rather than proposing a single shared currency, the Digital Amero framework asks a broader set of questions: What would a shared digital payments layer look like? Could stablecoins or central bank digital currencies serve as interoperable instruments across borders? How would distributed ledger technology change the calculus of monetary coordination?

This is not conspiracy theory territory. The idea originates from economists, policy researchers, and financial strategists who see an opportunity in the convergence of stablecoin regulation, blockchain settlement infrastructure, and deepening trade integration under agreements like USMCA.

In The Amero, author Che-Sun Lee treats the concept as a lens through which to examine the future of money itself—across borders, across technologies, and across the rapidly shifting landscape of global finance.

A New Moment

Why the Amero Debate Is Returning

For years, the Amero concept existed on the margins of economic policy discussion. It was raised occasionally in academic journals, debated in Canadian policy circles, and sometimes mischaracterized by conspiracy theorists. But something has changed.

The rapid development of digital currency infrastructure has made questions about monetary coordination newly urgent. Specifically, three converging forces are bringing the Amero debate back into serious conversation:

  • Stablecoin regulation is accelerating. The United States is moving toward a federal framework for payment stablecoins through legislation like the GENIUS Act. Canada and Mexico are each developing their own regulatory approaches to digital assets. The result is a patchwork of rules that could either fragment or integrate North American digital finance.
  • Central bank digital currencies are in active development worldwide. The digital euro, digital yuan, and numerous other CBDC projects are forcing every central bank to consider its digital strategy. The Bank of Canada, the Federal Reserve, and the Bank of Mexico are all evaluating their options.
  • Blockchain-based settlement is becoming real infrastructure. Technologies like XRP and cross-border payment networks are demonstrating that real-time, low-cost international settlement is technically feasible at scale. This changes the economic argument for monetary coordination.

Taken together, these developments mean the question is no longer whether digital money will reshape North American finance, but how—and whether coordination would serve all three nations better than fragmentation.

Technology & Policy

Stablecoins, CBDCs, and Blockchain Infrastructure

Understanding the Digital Amero framework requires understanding the three technological pillars it rests on: stablecoins, central bank digital currencies, and blockchain infrastructure.

Stablecoins are digital tokens pegged to fiat currencies, most commonly the U.S. dollar. They are already handling hundreds of billions of dollars in transaction volume and have become essential infrastructure for cryptocurrency markets, cross-border payments, and increasingly for mainstream financial services. The GENIUS Act represents the United States' most significant attempt to bring payment stablecoins under a clear regulatory framework, establishing reserve requirements, transparency standards, and issuer qualifications.

Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) represent the other end of the spectrum: state-issued digital money. The European Central Bank is advancing the digital euro. China has deployed the digital yuan in pilot programs across major cities. In North America, all three central banks have studied CBDC designs, though none has committed to a full launch. The choices each country makes about CBDCs will directly shape the feasibility of any cross-border digital coordination.

Blockchain and distributed ledger technology provide the infrastructure layer. Networks designed for institutional settlement—including those used in cross-border payment corridors—demonstrate that programmable, transparent, near-instant settlement across jurisdictions is no longer theoretical. This infrastructure is what makes a Digital Amero framework technically conceivable in a way it was not even a decade ago.

The intersection of these three elements is where the Digital Amero concept gains its analytical power. It is not about any one technology, but about how they combine to create new possibilities for monetary architecture.

The North American Context

Canada, the United States, and Mexico

The Amero concept does not exist in a vacuum. It is rooted in the specific economic, political, and financial realities of North America—three countries bound together by geography, trade, and an increasingly complex web of financial flows.

Trade integration under USMCA. The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement formalized one of the world's largest trading blocs. Goods, services, and capital move across these borders at enormous scale. Yet the financial infrastructure that supports this trade—payments, settlement, currency exchange—remains fragmented across three separate national systems with different currencies, different regulations, and different technological capabilities.

Cross-border payments remain expensive and slow. Despite decades of financial innovation, sending money between the United States and Mexico or between Canada and the United States still involves correspondent banking chains, multi-day settlement, and significant fees. Remittance corridors between the U.S. and Mexico alone represent tens of billions of dollars annually, with costs that disproportionately affect lower-income senders. Technologies like XRP-based payment solutions are beginning to address this, but systemic improvement requires more than private-sector innovation.

Different digital currency approaches. Each country is charting its own course. The United States is focused on stablecoin regulation and private-sector innovation. Canada has conducted extensive CBDC research through the Bank of Canada. Mexico has taken a cautious regulatory approach to digital assets while dealing with large-scale remittance flows that could benefit from digital infrastructure.

The question at the heart of the Digital Amero framework is whether these three nations would be better served by coordinating their digital financial strategies or by continuing to develop them independently. There is no simple answer, but the question itself deserves rigorous examination.

Setting the Record Straight

Why This Is Not a Conspiracy Theory

Any serious discussion of the Amero must address the conspiracy theories that have grown around the concept. In the mid-2000s, online speculation linked the Amero to claims about a secret "North American Union" being planned without public consent. These claims were unfounded and are not what The Amero is about.

The intellectual foundation for the Amero concept comes from entirely different sources:

  • Herbert Grubel's 1999 proposal. The economist Herbert Grubel, a professor at Simon Fraser University, published a formal proposal for a North American monetary union titled The Case for the Amero. His argument was grounded in optimal currency area theory—the same economic framework that informed the creation of the euro. Grubel's work was peer-reviewed, published through the Fraser Institute, and discussed in mainstream economic journals.
  • Academic literature on optimal currency areas. The question of whether geographically and economically linked nations should share monetary infrastructure has been a legitimate field of economic study since Robert Mundell's Nobel Prize-winning work in the 1960s. The Amero concept is a direct extension of this research tradition.
  • Policy research and institutional analysis. Think tanks, central bank researchers, and international economists have explored various forms of North American monetary cooperation over the decades. These analyses are available in the research section of this site.

The distinction matters. Conspiracy theories deal in hidden agendas and secret plans. The Amero framework, as explored in Che-Sun Lee's book and on this site, deals in transparent analysis of economic trends, technological possibilities, and policy options. Readers are encouraged to examine the evidence, consult the primary sources, and form their own conclusions.
